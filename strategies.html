Portfolio Size

How many stocks should you hold?

I recommend a minimum of 10 stocks per strategy. That’s where diversification starts to meaningfully reduce single-stock risk while still capturing the momentum or value signal. With fewer than 10, one bad position can seriously damage your year.

For larger portfolios, 25 stocks is optimal. My backtests show the Sharpe ratio peaks at 25 — beyond that, you dilute the factor exposure. The difference between 10 and 25 is modest in raw CAGR (~2%), but risk-adjusted returns improve and drawdowns become more manageable.

The Three Strategies

How each strategy works

Strategy 1

Value Strategy — 18.61% CAGR

I rank every stock in the market using six fundamental metrics: P/E, P/S, P/B, P/CF, EV/EBITDA, and Shareholder Yield. Combine them into a single composite score (VC2). Buy the cheapest. Rebalance.

I exclude financial stocks because P/B and EV/EBITDA aren’t meaningful for banks and insurers. Stocks with negative earnings are also removed.

This strategy performs best when including small caps — market cap above $200M is the sweet spot that balances liquidity with the small-cap value premium.

Strategy 2

Momentum + Value — 21.19% CAGR

Start with the Value strategy. Take the cheapest 10% of stocks by composite score. Then, within that cheap decile, rank by 6-month price momentum and buy the top ones.

This combines two of the strongest factors in finance — value and momentum. You’re buying cheap stocks that are already moving up, avoiding value traps.

Like the Value strategy, this works best with $200M+ market cap, and excludes financials and negative earnings.

Strategy 3

Intermediate Momentum — 21.7% CAGR

Based on Novy-Marx (2012). Instead of looking at recent returns (which tend to reverse), I use returns from months 7–12 — the “intermediate” momentum window that persists across time and markets.

The formula: 12-month return minus 6-month return. Buy the top stocks. Rebalance.

Unlike value, momentum works better on larger, more liquid stocks. I use $2B+ market cap — closely matching the universe where the original research was conducted.

How I invest

Why I run all three

Running all three lowers my overall portfolio variance and improves the Sharpe ratio. Value and momentum are negatively correlated — when one underperforms, the other picks up the slack. Combining them smooths out returns and makes the portfolio easier to hold through drawdowns.

That’s the beauty of systematic investing: I don’t need to think, analyse, or second-guess. The rules run themselves. I run all three with real capital, and the systematic side requires almost zero time — just one rebalance per year.

Rebalance frequency

Monthly rebalance is more effective

My backtests show that monthly rebalance outperforms annual by ~3–3.5% CAGR, with significantly lower drawdowns (−18% vs −30%). The momentum signal decays over time — refreshing it monthly captures more of the effect.

The catch: more frequent rebalancing means higher turnover, which triggers short-term capital gains tax in many jurisdictions. If short-term gains are taxed higher than long-term (e.g. the US), the tax drag can eat most of the extra return.

Rule of thumb: same tax rate for both? Monthly is clearly better. Higher short-term rate? Annual may win after tax. Choose based on your tax regime.
